Claude's response

What happens to a 5-second clip

Step 1. Decode the audio: about 121 ms, VAD included. - Whatever format the user uploads (M4A, MP3, WebM and so on) is converted to 16 kHz mono float32 with faster_whisper.decode_audio. - Silero VAD (voice activity detection) then checks for speech. A clip of 10 s or less stays as one segment. Longer clips are cut into 1–10 s pieces.

Step 2. Make the log-mel spectrogram (counted inside the encoder time). - The audio becomes a log-mel spectrogram at 100 frames per second. This is the image of the sound that Whisper reads. - It is padded to Whisper's 30-second window, even though the clip is only 5 s.

Step 3. Run the Whisper encoder once: 1,225 ms, 74% of the total. - The frozen small.en encoder, run in int8 on the CPU, outputs one 768-number vector per 20 ms. - This single pass feeds both branches. That is the key design point: one encoder, two uses. - It is slow because it processes the full 30 s of padded input. A 10 s window would be faster, but it raised WER by 5.85 points, so 30 s was kept by a pre-set rule.

Step 4a. Sound branch: 2 ms. - Only the valid frames are kept: 5 s ÷ 0.02 s = 250 frames, so padding is dropped. - The small attention-pooling head (0.27 M parameters) learns which frames matter, such as a shout or a laugh, and outputs 7 probabilities: p_sound.

Step 4b. Text branch: 273 ms decoder + 33 ms RoBERTa. - Whisper's decoder turns the same encoder output into a transcript, using greedy decoding. - RoBERTa-base (fp32) reads the transcript and outputs 7 probabilities: p_text.

Step 5. Calibrate and fuse: about 0 ms. - Each branch is temperature-scaled so its confidences are honest: T_sound = 0.814, T_text = 1.009. - The two are combined in log space with w = 0.5, a weighted geometric mean, which gives the final 7 probabilities. - Disagreement flag: raised if the two branches pick different labels and both are at least 40% confident. - If the transcript is empty, the text branch becomes uniform and the sound branch decides.

Step 6. Show the result. - The page shows the label with all 7 probabilities, a "How it sounds" card, a "What was said" card with the transcript, and the flag if raised. - The audio is never stored.

Total: 1,649 ms median, 1.40 GB peak memory. This meets the 2.0 s target but misses the 1.2 s stretch goal.

How to say it in 30 seconds

"The audio is decoded to 16 kHz and turned into a log-mel spectrogram. One pass of a frozen Whisper encoder serves two branches. A small attention head reads the encoder frames for how it sounds. Whisper's decoder makes a transcript that RoBERTa reads for what was said. Both outputs are temperature-calibrated and fused in log space, and a flag goes up if they confidently disagree. It takes 1.65 s on a laptop CPU. Three quarters of that is the Whisper encoder, because it pads every clip to 30 seconds. The trained heads take only 35 ms together."

Likely follow-up: "How would you make it faster?" Two options. Use a shorter window for short clips, but only with a better ASR model, since the 10 s window cost 5.85 WER points. Or use distil-Whisper. Speeding up the heads would not help, because they take only 35 ms.

Claude's response

Honest, but incomplete. About 3/10. You conceded the point and stopped there, but the question asks you to defend the decision. Admitting the clips are no longer blind is the right ending, not the whole answer.

What a full defence covers

  1. What the 28 clips actually did. They exposed a problem: fused 35.7% against text-only 60.7%. They were not used to fit the new weight.
  2. What the weight was fitted on. The 155 MELD dev clips from speakers outside the main six, MELD's closest stand-in for new voices. The old weight of 0.75 came from all of dev, which is 86% main cast, so it trusted the sound branch too much for unfamiliar voices.
  3. The MELD test set was never touched. Test wF1 moving from 54.5 to 55.7 was a consequence of the refit, not the target.
  4. The choice was made by rule. A grid of 0.00–1.00 in steps of 0.05, scored by wF1, with ties going to the smaller w.
  5. Stability was checked. Under resampling the weight stayed between 0.50 and 0.70. The 36 never-heard speakers alone gave 0.80, but that swung from 0.30 to 0.80, so it was rejected as too noisy.
  6. Strongest point: 0.50 is not the best weight for your own clips. Text-only scored 60.7% on them, and the deployed w = 0.50 scored 57.1%. If you had tuned on your clips, you would have chosen text-only. This is the clearest evidence the weight wasn't fitted to them.
  7. One parameter can't overfit much. Fusion has one weight plus two temperatures.
  8. Then the concession, which you already had: the 28 clips are labelled non-blind wherever they appear, and the fix is a fresh blind set from new speakers.

A tighter version to say aloud

"The own clips exposed a problem, but the new weight wasn't fitted on them. The old weight of 0.75 came from a dev set that's 86% main cast, so it over-trusted sound for new voices. I refit by a fixed grid rule on the 155 dev clips from speakers outside the main six. The weight was stable at 0.5 to 0.7 under resampling, and the MELD test set was never used. If I'd tuned on my own clips I'd have picked text-only, which scored 60.7% there versus 57.1% for the deployed weight. Still, those clips are no longer blind, and I say so everywhere they appear. In both models the training loss keeps falling, but the dev score peaks early and then drops. That is overfitting. After the peak, the model is memorising the training set instead of learning patterns that carry over to new clips.

What it tells you

  1. Both models run out of useful data quickly. 10k clips is small, so training longer doesn't help.
  2. The text model overfits faster, for two reasons. - All 125 M of RoBERTa's parameters are being fine-tuned. It already understands language, so it learns the task almost immediately and then starts memorising. The sound head has only 0.27 M parameters on frozen features, so it takes longer to memorise. - The text training set holds both the gold and the Whisper version of the same utterances. So one epoch already shows most utterances twice.
  3. Picking the best checkpoint mattered. It saved about 3.5 points on sound (53.1 vs 49.6 at the last epoch) and about 2 on text (50.3 vs 48.4). - Sound used early stopping with patience 6. It stopped at epoch 10 and restored the epoch-4 weights. - Text ran all 6 epochs and kept the best one, which was epoch 1.
  4. The learning-rate schedules hardly mattered. - The sound head stopped so early that cosine decay had barely started: the learning rate was still about 0.87e-3. - The text model's best checkpoint came at step 525, just after the 315-step warm-up ended.

Why select the text model on Whisper transcripts, not gold

  • Select on the condition you deploy in. In the app, the text model never sees clean subtitles. It reads Whisper's transcripts, which have 26% WER on dev and 33% on test.
  • Gold dev text would reward a model that is good at clean, subtitle-style sentences. That isn't the input it will get, and it could pick a different epoch.
  • Noisy dev text also shows overfitting to clean training text earlier.

Likely follow-ups

  • "Why train for 6 epochs if epoch 1 is best?" The recipe was fixed in the spec before training, using standard fine-tuning settings, and saving the best checkpoint protected against it. With hindsight, 2–3 epochs would have been enough and cheaper.
  • "Why no validation loss?" Selection used dev wF1 directly, because that is the reported metric. Weighted loss and wF1 can disagree, so choosing on loss could pick a worse model by the metric that matters.

A tighter version to say aloud

"Both models overfit early. Training loss keeps falling while dev wF1 peaks and then drops: epoch 4 for the sound head, epoch 1 for the text model. Text overfits faster because all of RoBERTa's 125 M parameters are fine-tuned. It already understands language, so it learns the task in one epoch and then memorises. The sound head is only 0.27 M parameters on frozen features. Keeping the best checkpoint saved 2 to 3.5 points. I selected the text model on Whisper transcripts because that is what it reads in deployment. Choosing on clean gold text would optimise for an input it never sees."

Claude's response

Good. About 7.5/10. You named one lever, backed it with the right numbers (61.5 vs 52.3, and 62.3 vs 55.5), called 7 points an upper bound, and raised latency yourself. Three points would make it much stronger.

1. The latency problem is bigger than "as long as it fits" - The Whisper encoder already takes 1.23 s of the 1.65 s total. That leaves only about 0.35 s of headroom under the 2 s target. - A bigger Whisper means a bigger encoder, which almost certainly breaks the budget on this CPU. - Distil-Whisper doesn't solve it. It shrinks the decoder, but your bottleneck is the encoder.

2. Changing the ASR model also breaks the sound branch (the sharpest point) - The sound head was trained on small.en's encoder features. A new ASR model means retraining the sound head on the new features. - The alternative is keeping small.en for sound and adding a second ASR model. That doubles the cost and gives up the one-pass design.

3. The 7 points is a ceiling you can't fully reach - Of the 7,761 test errors, 4,029 are insertions. - MELD's references are subtitles that drop fillers, laughter and other speakers' lines. Part of the 33% WER is a style mismatch, not real recognition errors. No ASR model will close that part.

Mention the alternative - On your own phone recordings the sound branch scores only 28.6%. - Recording-condition augmentation (phone-mic simulation, room reverberation, noise) targets that directly. - It adds zero latency, because it changes training only, and it fits in two weeks. - So you can answer: "for MELD-like audio, ASR is the biggest lever; for real users on phones, augmentation is the fastest win."

A tighter version to say aloud

"The biggest lever on the numbers is speech recognition. Text drops from 61.5 on gold transcripts to 52.3 on Whisper's, and fused with gold text would reach 62.3 against 55.5 today. That 7 points is a ceiling, though. Over half the WER is insertions against subtitle-style references, which no ASR model fixes. And a bigger Whisper means a bigger encoder, which is already 1.2 of my 1.65 seconds, and I'd have to retrain the sound head on its features. So with two weeks I'd do recording-condition augmentation for the sound head first. It costs no latency and targets the 28.6% on phone recordings, which is the weakness real users would hit. Then I'd test a better ASR model under the 2-second budget."
